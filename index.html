Advanced Qualitative Methods

The Field Researcher's Dilemma

Eight districts. One theory. Your judgment.

01

You are, for now, a figment of your own ambitions. A PhD candidate, notebook in hand, inspired by one paper above all others — Hanne Fjelde's landmark study of political party strength and electoral violence, published in the Journal of Peace Research. Fjelde showed, across all national elections from 1946 to 2010, that strong party organizations reduce the risk of electoral violence. The statistics are compelling. But statistics, you keep thinking, cannot tell you why.

You want to go deeper. You want to design a qualitative study that tests and refines Fjelde's argument from the ground up — district by district, interview by interview. And to do that, you first need to make the most consequential decision in any qualitative researcher's career: which cases do you study?

Fjelde's Argument, in Brief

Fjelde argues that strong political party organizations reduce electoral violence through two mechanisms:

1. They reduce the incentive to use violence. Strong parties can mobilize voters through organizational outreach — local branches, programmatic appeals, stable networks. Violence is costly and risky; parties with better tools don't need it.

2. They constrain individual actors from deploying violence. Institutionalized parties impose formal and informal rules on their members. Leaders and grassroots actors are less free to act unilaterally — the organization itself acts as a brake on violence.

Your task is to design a qualitative study that can test and refine this argument at the voting district level, where politics plays out in streets, polling stations, and party offices.

Which District Would Make the Best Single Case?

Sometimes one case teaches more than two.

Not all research questions call for a comparison. Sometimes, a single well-chosen case can teach you more than two mediocre ones side by side. The art lies in knowing what kind of case you're dealing with — and what kind of analytical leverage it gives you.

Stage 5 — The Uniqueness Problem · Part A

Is This District Really Unique?

"You have to understand — this place is different from everywhere else."

06

You've been in the field for three weeks. The interviews are going well — perhaps too well. Again and again, your research participants offer a variation of the same observation: "You have to understand, this district is different from everywhere else. What happens here can't be compared to other places. Our history, our community — it's unique."

It's a seductive claim. And it may not be entirely wrong. But before you accept it — before you let it reshape your design — you need to stop and ask a harder question.

Before you accept that this district is exceptional, investigate the claim.

It is a well-documented feature of human psychology that people across the world believe their own communities are unlike any other. This is not evidence of structural uniqueness — it is a near-universal feature of local identity. The fact that your research participants believe their district is unique tells you something important about community identity. It does not, by itself, tell you whether the district actually deviates from broader patterns.

Your task is to treat the uniqueness claim as something to be assessed carefully, not a finding to be accepted.

Stage 5 — The Uniqueness Problem · Part B

What If the Uniqueness Claim Holds?

Your comparison just got complicated.

06

Suppose your investigation reveals that the uniqueness claim has merit. After checking the secondary literature, comparing structural indicators across districts, and triangulating your interviews, you are forced to conclude: this district does have a distinctive feature — one that is not shared by your comparison case, and that may independently explain the pattern of violence you observe.

This is a genuine problem for your comparative design. If the two cases differ on more than just party strength, then the difference in violence cannot be straightforwardly attributed to the IV.

Stage 6 — A Research Design Crisis

The Ground Shifts Beneath Your Feet

The violence wasn't what you thought it was.

07

Months into your fieldwork in [high-violence district], you make a discovery that changes everything.

You've been meticulously documenting incidents of electoral violence — attacks on party offices, intimidation of voters, clashes between political supporters. The case looked exactly as advertised: high levels of violence during the electoral period, concentrated in the political spaces your theory predicts. But as your interviews deepen and your local contacts multiply, a different story begins to emerge.

A large share of the violence — more than you initially estimated, possibly a majority — was not perpetrated by political party supporters at all. It was carried out by criminal gangs who, for reasons of their own, adopted the visual and rhetorical trappings of political affiliation. They wore party colors. They used party slogans. But they were motivated by profit, not electoral outcomes. By your working definition of electoral violence, much of what you documented doesn't qualify.

You have invested too much — in time, in relationships, in your PhD timeline — to change your case selection. What do you do?
